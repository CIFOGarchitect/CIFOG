FUNDAMENTALS

Interoperability means this: different people, with different radios, can still talk to each other when it matters.

The escalation ladder

Work from the top down; stop at the first method that works:

  1. Face to face, where possible.
  2. Your local, everyday method (cell, mesh, home).
  3. A shared system your group already uses.
  4. A mutual-aid channel both sides have (the Vanguard channels).
  5. A gateway or patch between two systems, if one is available.
  6. A cache radio handed to someone who needs one.
  7. A relay: a person carries the message.

Rules that make it work

  • PACE: Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency — always have a backup to the backup, and know what it is before you need it.
  • Same names in every radio. Every radio programs these channels with identical names. Different names means confusion, not interoperability.
  • A tone is not privacy. DPL and CTCSS only filter what your speaker plays. Anyone on the frequency hears you.
  • Say who you are first. Group and identifier before your message, every time.
  • Strict net control, someone always listening. One station runs the net; one keeps watch on WARPIGEON during any activation.
  • How to read a channel entry: callsign, frequency, tone/DPL, mode, purpose. Example: WARPIGEON — 151.625 MHz, DPL 023, analog narrowband, command and calling.

I. OPERATIONAL DOCTRINE

1.1 Radio Etiquette

  • LISTEN FIRST: Monitor for 30 seconds before transmitting.
  • 1-SECOND RULE: Key the mic, wait 1 second, then speak.
  • PLAIN LANGUAGE: No 10-codes. Use: Copy, Affirmative, Negative, Wait, Say Again.

1.2 LCAN Reporting Format

Use this structure for all status updates:

  • L — Location: Your current position.
  • C — Conditions: Fire, Medical, Flood, or All Clear.
  • A — Actions: What you are doing now.
  • N — Needs: Resources required.
Worked example: "WARPIGEON, this is [group] [identifier] — Location: north trailhead parking lot. Conditions: All Clear. Actions: moving to checkpoint two. Needs: none."

GROUP ROLES & NET CONTROL

  • OVERLORD (net control): Runs the net. Declares all-calls and silence, broadcasts Vectors, lifts silence, stands the net down. One net control per net.
  • Monitor: Keeps a listening watch on WARPIGEON during any activation and logs traffic.
  • Logger: Keeps the written log: times, stations, messages, problems.
  • Tech: Keeps every group radio programmed alike, charged, and tested. Owns the cache.
  • Every member: Knows the plan, keeps their keycard current, checks in when called.

Net-control checklist

  • Get briefed. Assign a monitor. Post the channel plan. Keep a log. Brief your relief. Document problems.

Adopting CIFOG as a group

Agree on the plan and its version, program every radio alike, name the roles and backups, set a test cadence, review changes together. When the guide version changes, re-print the keycards.

When groups merge

Agree on three things before operating together: one net control, one common channel plan, and how callsigns work across groups. Then operate as one net.

ACTIVATION & PROCEDURES

Activation sequence

  1. Need identified — a member or net control calls for activation.
  2. Verify a common channel — every station confirms it has the current plan before anyone moves.
  3. Check in on WARPIGEON, group and identifier first.
  4. Net control posts the working channel(s) and the plan.
  5. Operate — net control directs traffic; all others wait for direction.
  6. Stand down — net control announces return to normal operations, stations acknowledge, activation is logged.

All-call (OVERLORD)

When OVERLORD declares an all-call, all stations switch to channel 15 (OVERLORD) and check in. Net control directs traffic from there.

Silence (BLACKOUT)

When OVERLORD declares silence, all stations cease transmitting immediately and monitor channel 16 (BLACKOUT) until OVERLORD lifts silence. The only exception is immediate life-safety traffic.

Vector procedure

OVERLORD broadcasts the Vector in plain language on the active net channel ("Vector 3"). Each station adds the Vector to today's seed and switches to the resulting channel. If the result is not a valid channel (1–16), disregard it and await a corrected Vector — do not guess, and do not wrap around.

Stand-down and after-action

Every activation and exercise ends twice: on the air (net control stands the net down) and on paper (a short hot wash: what worked, what didn't, what changes).

Testing cadence

Brief weekly check-in on WARPIGEON; monthly net exercising one PACE fallback; cache radios checked monthly.

Troubleshooting

Can't reach the group? Check channel, tone, battery, antenna, position. Fall back per PACE. Relay through another station. Then fall to contingency at the next 3-3-3 window. Work the ladder — never sit on a dead channel hoping.

PROGRAMMING & EQUIPMENT

  • Programming template: Every group radio gets the full Vanguard zone with identical names. Family PRIVATE channels are added on top — never instead of the base. Verify by read-back after programming and after any change.
  • Group cache: A box of charged spares, programmed identically, stored in a known place, with a named keeper and a checkout/return log. Checked monthly.
  • Asset list: One page — member, callsign, radio(s), bands, mobile or portable, vehicle rig, notes. Update when gear changes.
  • Amateur radio bridge: Licensed amateurs and volunteer groups are a real resource: wider nets, repeaters, experienced operators. Know your local clubs and repeaters through current directories. Transmitting on amateur bands requires an amateur license.

READINESS SELF-ASSESSMENT

Grade your group honestly. Any unchecked box is your next job.

  • Written plan adopted, with a named net control and backup.
  • Every radio programmed alike, names identical, tested on the air.
  • Keycards printed, current, matching the guide version.
  • Cache charged, tested, keeper named.
  • Every PACE level exercised at least once in practice.
  • Test cadence running: weekly check-in, monthly net.
  • Log habit: activations and exercises logged.
  • After-action habit: hot wash after every exercise and activation.
  • Version control: the group knows which version it runs.
  • Fallback for total radio failure: runners, meeting points, the relay step.

WATCH-OUTS

  • Congestion: Too many voices on one channel. Net control directs; everyone else waits.
  • Unfamiliar radio: You cannot run what you have never practiced on.
  • Unclear assignments: If you don't know your job, ask before transmitting.
  • Dead zones: Buildings, garages, and valleys kill signal. Move, elevate, or relay.
  • No net control: Two nets on one channel with nobody directing is not a net.
  • Different words: Different terms for the same thing cause confusion. Plain language, always.
  • Dead batteries and untested gear: Check before you need it.
  • Assuming privacy: DPL and CTCSS are not encryption. Anyone on the frequency hears you.
  • Programming at the incident: Multiple people reprogramming creates mismatched radios.
  • Too few channels: If the working channel is busy, net control moves traffic — don't pile on.

GLOSSARY

  • Cache: Spare radios and equipment held in reserve for issue.
  • Callsign: The name a station uses on the air.
  • CSQ: Carrier squelch — no tone or code; any signal opens the speaker.
  • CTCSS: Continuous Tone-Coded Squelch System — sub-audible tone squelch. Filtering, not privacy.
  • Direct / simplex: Radio-to-radio without a repeater (talk-around).
  • DPL: Digital Private Line — digital squelch code. Filtering, not privacy, not encryption.
  • Gateway / patch: A connection between two systems or channels so users on each can hear each other.
  • GMRS: General Mobile Radio Service — licensed UHF service for families and groups.
  • ICS / NIMS: Incident Command System / National Incident Management System — the standard framework for managing incidents.
  • Keycard: The printed card carrying the seed table and status codes, kept with the radio.
  • LCAN: Location, Conditions, Actions, Needs — the status report format.
  • MURS: Multi-Use Radio Service — license-free VHF short-range service.
  • Muster: A gathering or check-in, on the air or in person.
  • Net control: The station directing traffic on a net (in CIFOG, OVERLORD).
  • NFM: Narrowband FM — the voice mode used by the Vanguard channels.
  • NIFOG: National Interoperability Field Operations Guide (CISA) — the national model CIFOG adapts for civilian use.
  • PACE: Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency — a layered plan so losing one method never leaves you with nothing.
  • Repeater: A station that receives and retransmits signals to extend range.
  • Seed: The daily number from the keycard, added to a broadcast Vector to find the target channel.
  • Vector: A number broadcast by OVERLORD; Vector + Seed = Channel.
  • VTAC / UTAC: National interoperability tactical channel names. V = VHF, U = UHF; TAC = tactical. In CIFOG's PACE these are monitor-only for civilians: listen, do not transmit.
